    How the numbers are chosen¶

    Once the network is fixed, every node and link needs numbers: how much customers buy, how long deliveries take, when to reorder, how big the factory is, what things cost. This page explains where each number comes from. The code is configure() in generator/chain.py.

    The main idea: customers first, everything else follows

    Customer demand is drawn first. It is then pushed up the network, so each site knows how much it must carry on a normal day. Stock levels, factory size, prices and freight are all worked out from that flow. A big shop therefore gets a big stock and a busy factory gets a big machine. Nothing is random on its own, so the numbers always fit together.

    0. Setup-wide settings¶

    Before any node is filled, a few settings are drawn once for the whole setup. They are stored in the configs table.

    • Industry: groceries (FMCG), electronics, medicines (pharma) or industrial spare parts. Each industry has its own number ranges (table below).
    • Stress or control. 80% of setups are stress setups: they may forecast badly, run price wars or aim for low stock levels, so failures happen. 20% are control setups: honest forecasts, normal prices and a 90%, 95% or 98% service target, the usual range in practice 3. The regime column tells them apart.
    • Season (70% of setups): demand goes up and down once a year, usually by up to ±50% (a few streams up to ±75%).
    • Growth: demand grows or shrinks by −10% to +20% a year.
    • Breakdown risk (60% of setups): some sites break down now and then.
    • Limited suppliers (50% of setups): some suppliers can run out.

    1. Customer demand¶

    Each customer stream gets:

    Number How it is chosen
    Average units per day Drawn around the industry's typical value (a log-normal spread)
    Orders per day Drawn from the industry's range. Average order size = units ÷ orders
    Order size Varies around its average. How much it varies depends on the industry (industrial orders are the lumpiest)
    Season and growth The setup's season and growth, with a small change per stream

    Customers arrive at random times (a Poisson process 1), faster in the busy season. A customer takes what is on the shelf. If the shelf is short, the rest of the order is lost. Customers never wait.

    2. Flow pushed upstream¶

    Each site's flow is the average number of units it must pass on per day:

    • A shop's flow = its customers' demand.
    • A warehouse's flow = the share of its children's flow that it supplies.
    • A factory's flow = what its warehouses take from it.
    • A supplier's flow = the factory's flow × units of raw material per product (the recipe, 1 to 3).

    A site with two suppliers splits its orders at random with fixed weights. The main supplier gets 50% to 90% of orders.

    3. Delivery times¶

    Every link gets a delivery time drawn from its industry's range (table below). Each shipment's actual time varies around that average (a gamma distribution), by an amount that is also drawn per setup. A factory needs all its raw materials, so its planning time is the longest of its inbound deliveries, plus the time to make a batch.

    4. Prices¶

    • Suppliers sell raw material at a price from the industry range.
    • A factory's cost per unit = raw materials + making cost.
    • The shop price is the factory cost × a markup. The markup is drawn per setup and split along the chain between the factory, the warehouses and the shop.
    • Stress setups may squeeze the markup almost to zero (a price war), so some of them lose money.
    • Holding cost: keeping one unit in stock for a year costs 10% to 40% of its value. The lower end matches the median measured by APQC across 6,468 organisations 5.
    • Freight: each delivery costs a fixed amount plus a small share (0.3% to 3%) of the value of the goods.

    5. Stock rule at every site¶

    Every factory, warehouse and shop follows the (s, S) rule, the standard textbook stock rule 2:

    Check stock every r days. If the stock position has fallen to the reorder level s or below, order enough to bring it back up to the order-up-to level S.

    The stock position is the stock on the shelf, plus what is already on its way, minus what is still owed to others.

    How s and S are worked out:

    1. Forecast. The planner's estimate of daily demand. In stress setups it is often too low (by up to about 50%). In control setups it is right on average.

    2. Cover for the delivery time. s must cover the forecast demand during the delivery time plus the review period, plus a safety margin for surprises:

      \[ s = \hat D\,(L + r) + z \sqrt{(L + r)\,\sigma_D^2 + \hat D^2\,\sigma_L^2} \]

      where \(\hat D\) is the forecast per day, \(L\) the delivery time, \(r\) the review period, \(\sigma_D^2\) and \(\sigma_L^2\) the variances of daily demand and delivery time, and \(z\) the safety factor set by the service target (for example 95% → z ≈ 1.64). This is the usual safety-stock formula 3. 3. Order size Q = S − s. Chosen so that ordering costs and holding costs are balanced, in the style of the economic order quantity (EOQ) 4, then allowed to vary. 4. Never too small for the children. A warehouse's s is at least the biggest order one of its children places, so a single child order never empties it by surprise.

    Every site starts the simulation with S units on the shelf.

    6. Factory capacity¶

    A factory makes one batch at a time. The batch size is set so that, on a normal day, the factory is busy 60% to 97% of the time. For comparison, Indian manufacturing as a whole ran at 74.6% of capacity in early 2026 6.

    Capacity is sized for normal demand. The busy season and demand growth can push a factory above its limit. How far is recorded in peak_load (above 1 means it cannot keep up at the peak).

    7. Limited suppliers¶

    In half of the setups, some suppliers are limited. They produce raw material at a fixed rate (3% to 67% above their normal flow) into a store of limited size. If factories need more than that for long enough, the supplier runs out.

    The store holds 7 to 30 days of flow, and at least enough to fill the largest single order a factory can ever send it. That second rule matters: in SupplyNetPy an order is shipped whole, so an order bigger than the whole store could never be filled and the factory would stop for good.

    8. Breakdowns¶

    In 60% of setups, each supplier, factory and warehouse (without its own customers) may break down, with a chance of 20% to 70% per site. A site that can break down does so every 180 to 1,500 days on average, and each breakdown lasts 2 to 30 days on average. The time between breakdowns is random (exponential). Breakdown lengths have a long tail, so a rare breakdown can last several months. For comparison, the 2021 fire at the Renesas Naka chip plant took about 97 days to recover from 7.

    During a breakdown a site takes no new orders. Shipments already on the road still arrive.

    The four industries¶

    The rules above are the same for every industry. Only the ranges change:

    Groceries (FMCG) Electronics Medicines (pharma) Industrial spare parts
    Typical demand per customer stream 40 units/day 8 units/day 15 units/day 2 units/day
    Customer orders per day 2–6 0.5–2 1–3 0.1–0.8 (few, large orders)
    Delivery time, supplier → factory 2–10 days 14–45 days 30–90 days 7–30 days
    Delivery time, factory → warehouse 1–5 days 5–30 days 3–10 days 3–14 days
    Delivery time, warehouse → shop 1–3 days 2–7 days 1–4 days 2–7 days
    Raw material price per unit 0.2–2 10–120 2–40 20–400
    Shop price ÷ factory cost 1.5–2.4 1.3–2.0 1.8–4.5 1.6–3.5
    Holding cost per year (share of value) 10%–30% 10%–40% 10%–30% 10%–35%
    Average service target 50%–99% 50%–98% 70%–99.5% 50%–98%
    Freight (share of goods value) 1%–3% 0.5%–2% 0.3%–1% 1%–3%
    Unit consumer pack device strip / pack part

    Money has no currency. Each setup uses its own price units, set by its raw-material price.

    Where these ranges come from

    The ranges are reasoned design choices, chosen to be plausible for each industry and wide enough to give variety. They are not fitted to data. The few that were checked against public figures are listed in Assumptions and limits.
